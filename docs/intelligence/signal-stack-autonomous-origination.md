The broker network is a filter for what the market has already seen. By the time a deal reaches a broker, the information asymmetry is gone. The institutional buyer who depends on broker relationships competes at the end of the information chain, not at its origin.

The signal stack reverses this position. It detects seller motivation before it becomes a listing, maps ownership change before it becomes public, and executes outreach before the process starts. This memo outlines the stack: the data layer, the agent layer, and the execution layer.

## The Information Asymmetry Problem

Traditional origination assumes the broker is the gatekeeper of deal flow. The assumption is obsolete. Seller signals exist in public data months before any broker engagement, and the buyer who reads those signals operates ahead of the market.

The signals are scattered across registries, filings, and professional footprints. An ownership filing, a hiring pattern, a capacity strain in operations. None of these are deal listings, and all of them precede listings.

The asymmetry is temporal. The buyer who detects the signal in week two negotiates without competition. The buyer who waits for the broker in week forty competes in an auction.

The difference is not skill; it is infrastructure.

## The Data Layer

The data layer collects and normalizes seller signals at scale. Company registries provide ownership structure, board changes, and capital events. Business filings expose financial trajectory.

Professional networks expose succession intent.

The collection is continuous, not periodic. A quarterly manual review misses the window; a continuous pipeline catches it. The system watches thousands of entities and scores each on motivation probability.

The scoring model is transparent. Ownership tenure beyond twenty years signals succession timing. Growth outpacing management infrastructure signals capacity strain.

Consolidation in the sector signals fear of worse terms later. Each signal carries weight, and the composite score ranks the outreach queue.

## The Agent Layer

Autonomous agents execute the monitoring loop without human intervention. Each agent owns a slice of the pipeline: one watches registries, one tracks professional footprints, one maintains the scoring model. The agents run continuously and report exceptions.

The agents do not replace judgment; they replace latency. The human principal reviews the ranked queue and makes the calls. The agents compress the time between signal and outreach from months to days.

The division of labor is explicit. Detection is mechanical and belongs to agents. Evaluation is analytical and belongs to the principal.

The stack fails when the two roles blur.

## Terminal-Based Execution

The stack executes from the terminal, not from a browser. Command-line tools pull the data, run the scoring, and generate the outreach queue. The workflow is scriptable, repeatable, and auditable.

Terminal-based execution gives the buyer velocity. A signal detected at 08:00 becomes a queued outreach at 08:05. The broker-based competitor reads the same signal at 08:00 and schedules a call for next week.

The velocity advantage compounds. Every deal closed through the stack funds the next iteration of the stack. The infrastructure is an asset that appreciates with each execution.

## Signal-to-Deal Conversion

The conversion from signal to deal follows a defined sequence. The signal stack identifies the target. The scoring model ranks the motivation.

The outreach engine personalizes the approach. The principal closes the conversation.

The conversion rate depends on specificity. A letter that references the owner's actual operation converts; a generic thesis does not. The stack generates the specificity from the data layer, which is why it outperforms manual outreach.

The economics are asymmetric. Two hundred targeted outreaches produce four conversations, and one conversation becomes a closed deal. The asset created exceeds the campaign cost by orders of magnitude.

No broker commission, no auction, no competitive process.

## The Competitive Moat

The signal stack is a moat because it compounds. Every signal logged, every outreach sent, and every deal closed improves the next iteration. The data layer accumulates, the scoring model refines, and the outreach engine learns what converts.

The moat is invisible to competitors. They see the closed deals but not the infrastructure behind them. They attribute the outcome to relationships while the stack produces it systematically.

The moat widens with time. A broker network is finite; the signal stack scales with compute. The buyer who builds the stack in year one operates at a structural advantage in year five.

## The Human Layer

The stack does not eliminate the human principal; it repositions them. The principal owns the relationship, the judgment, and the close. The stack owns the detection, the ranking, and the outreach mechanics.

The division is explicit. Detection is mechanical and belongs to the agents. Evaluation is analytical and belongs to the principal.

The stack fails when the two roles blur. The principal who works with the stack operates at higher leverage. The same team covers ten times the targets with the same headcount.

The constraint shifts from capacity to judgment, which is the right constraint.

## Adoption Barriers

Adoption faces three barriers. The first is data access, since many registries require licenses and data quality varies across jurisdictions. The cost of clean data is the real entry barrier.

The second is engineering capability. Building the stack requires skills that traditional M&A teams lack. Most boutique firms have neither data engineers nor agent developers.

The third is management perception. Relationship-based origination reads as personal and worth protecting, while infrastructure reads as dehumanizing. The reality is the reverse: infrastructure gives relationships room to operate.

## Timing

Timing decides the outcome. The wealth transfer runs now, and the window closes within two to three years. The buyer who builds the stack this year operates through the entire transfer.

Every quarter without a stack is a quarter of lost asymmetry. Competitors automate, brokers automate, and owners digitize their processes. The information advantages erode from every direction.

The rational position is to build now. The cost is engineering time and data licenses. The value is access to deals that never reach the market, and the calculation is one-sided.

## Edge Cases

The stack fails in predictable patterns. The false signal produces outreach to an owner with no intention to sell, and the relationship starts from a deficit. The mitigation is advisor confirmation before direct contact.

The crowded target attracts multiple buyers to the same owners. The mitigation is specificity: reference the business's actual operation, not the sector's thesis. The timing miss produces outreach after the engagement letter, and the deal becomes process-bound.

The data error produces outreach to the wrong person or entity. The mitigation is verification before contact. Each failure is logged, and the stack recalibrates.

## Summary

The broker network is the end of the information chain. The signal stack is its origin. Data scraping, autonomous agents, and terminal-based execution convert public signals into proprietary deal flow before the market sees it.

The asymmetry is temporal, and the stack owns the temporal advantage. The buyer who builds it operates ahead of every process. The buyer who depends on relationships competes at the end of the chain, where the best deals are already gone.
